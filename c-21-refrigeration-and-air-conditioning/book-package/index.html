State Requirements

Nevada's C-21 Refrigeration and Air Conditioning classification allows a license holder to perform work authorized under the C-21 subclassifications.

The scope includes refrigeration systems used to control temperatures below 50°F in refrigerated rooms and insulated spaces, together with related insulation, ducts, blowers, registers, humidity controls, thermostatic controls, and specified gas and electrical connections.

The C-21 scope also includes installation, repair, service, and maintenance of air-conditioning equipment, machinery, units, and related ductwork using evaporation, refrigeration, boilers, or combustion to control temperatures in occupied structures.

Additional work within the classification includes applicable sheet metal fabrication and installation, repair and replacement of existing refrigeration and air-conditioning system components, solar equipment used for air-temperature control, chilled-water systems, and piping used to transfer hot water for temperature control.

The broad scope explains why the C-21 examination covers refrigeration, air conditioning, sheet metal and ducts, hydronics, fuel systems, solar, chilled water, controls, load calculations, ventilation, and safety.

Test Information and Study Materials

A productive C-21 study plan should begin with the highest-weight examination categories and then expand to the supporting subjects.

Fuel Systems should receive significant attention because it accounts for 10 questions. Review natural gas piping, sizing, pressure, regulators, appliance connections, shutoffs, testing, combustion air, venting, and related requirements using NFPA 54.

Refrigerants and Refrigeration should include the refrigeration cycle, pressure-temperature relationships, compressors, condensers, evaporators, metering devices, refrigerants, superheat, subcooling, system operation, and troubleshooting fundamentals.

Piping preparation should include materials, fittings, routing, joining methods, insulation, supports, pressure drop, refrigerant lines, hydronic piping, and proper installation practices.

Load Calculations should be practiced rather than simply read. Candidates should become comfortable identifying design conditions, evaluating building loads, and working through the calculation process used for small commercial HVAC systems.

Ducts should include sheet-metal and flexible duct construction, joints, reinforcement, supports, airflow, leakage considerations, installation, and coordination with HVAC equipment.

Controls should include thermostats, relays, contactors, pressure controls, safety devices, dampers, valves, and the basic relationship between electrical controls and mechanical-system operation.

Chimneys, Flues and Vents should be studied alongside fuel systems. Candidates should understand venting principles, connectors, clearances, terminations, appliance venting, and combustion-product removal.

A/C and Heat Pump Equipment and Furnaces and Heaters should include system components, operation, installation, airflow, fuel considerations, electrical controls, and troubleshooting concepts.

Chillers and Hydronics should include pumps, valves, coils, piping, cooling towers, chilled-water circulation, hot-water systems, and the relationship between water flow and temperature control.

Evaporative Cooling should include airflow, pads or media, pumps, water distribution, temperature reduction, and system applications. The Evaporative Air Conditioning Handbook can support this preparation.

Solar should include collectors, piping, storage, circulation, energy transfer, and equipment used in solar-assisted temperature-control systems.

Testing, Adjusting and Balancing should include airflow, system performance, measurement, commissioning concepts, and recognizing conditions that prevent proper equipment operation.

Ventilation and Exhausts should include airflow, exhaust systems, mechanical ventilation, ductwork, equipment, and code-based requirements.

Safety should be learned before test day because OSHA is not allowed into the examination. Candidates should review electrical hazards, ladders, personal protective equipment, tools, material handling, fall protection, and other construction safety subjects.

More on test Information and Study Materials

The study-only references should gradually transition from reading material to active recall. After reviewing a topic, close the book and explain the system, calculation, or procedure without looking at the page. Then use practice questions to test retention.

The Uniform Mechanical Code and NFPA 54 should be handled differently because they are available during the examination. Practice targeted lookups using the table of contents, index, definitions, chapters, and tables until the books are familiar.

Timed practice is especially important. With 85 questions in three hours, candidates should know when a lookup is worthwhile and when it is better to answer from technical knowledge and continue.

How does the Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Contractor (C-21) exam work?

The Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Contractor (C-21) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

  • Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
  • The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
  • PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
  • The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
