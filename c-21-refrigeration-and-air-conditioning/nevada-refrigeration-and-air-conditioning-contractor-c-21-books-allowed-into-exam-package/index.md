## State Requirements

Nevada's **C-21 Refrigeration and Air Conditioning** classification covers a broad range of refrigeration, HVAC, and related mechanical work.

The scope includes refrigeration systems used to control temperatures below 50°F in refrigerated rooms and insulated spaces, together with related insulation, ducts, blowers, registers, humidity controls, thermostatic controls, and specified gas and electrical connections.

The classification also includes installation, repair, service, and maintenance of air-conditioning equipment and related ductwork used to control temperatures in occupied structures.

C-21 work can also include sheet metal associated with HVAC systems, repair and replacement of refrigeration and air-conditioning components, solar equipment used for air-temperature control, chilled-water systems, and hot-water piping used for temperature control.

The broad scope explains why the examination includes refrigeration, air conditioning, fuel systems, ductwork, load calculations, piping, controls, hydronics, chillers, solar, ventilation, safety, and other mechanical-system topics.

The C-21 trade examination is one part of the Nevada contractor licensing process. Applicants must also satisfy applicable qualification, application, business and law, and other state licensing requirements before a contractor license is issued.

## Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada C-21 preparation plan should connect each major subject with the approved code reference most likely to support it.

**Fuel Systems** should receive substantial attention because this is the largest exam category. Review natural gas piping, sizing, system pressure, regulators, appliance connections, testing, shutoff requirements, combustion air, and venting. NFPA 54 should be used regularly for this portion of preparation.

**Refrigerants and Refrigeration** should include the refrigeration cycle, compressors, condensers, evaporators, metering devices, refrigerant behavior, pressure-temperature relationships, superheat, subcooling, and general system operation.

The Uniform Mechanical Code can support code-based refrigeration questions, but candidates should still build strong technical understanding because the code is not a substitute for knowing refrigeration fundamentals.

**Piping** should include materials, fittings, joining methods, supports, insulation, routing, refrigerant lines, hydronic piping, and proper installation practices. Candidates should understand how poor piping can affect pressure drop, oil return, system capacity, and equipment performance.

**Ducts** should include airflow, sheet-metal and flexible duct systems, supports, joints, sizing concepts, installation, leakage considerations, and coordination with air-conditioning equipment.

**Load Calculations** should be practiced as a technical skill. Candidates should understand the purpose of heating and cooling load calculations and how building characteristics, occupancy, internal gains, outdoor conditions, and system design influence equipment sizing.

**Controls** should include thermostats, relays, contactors, pressure controls, valves, dampers, sensors, and safety controls. Candidates should understand how these devices start, stop, regulate, and protect HVAC equipment.

**A/C and Heat Pump Equipment** should be studied as complete systems. Review compressors, coils, airflow, refrigerant movement, controls, defrost considerations, and equipment operation.

**Furnaces and Heaters** should include fuel supply, combustion air, burners, controls, heat exchangers, venting, and safe operation. Questions involving gas-fired equipment may require both the UMC and NFPA 54 depending on the subject.

**Chimneys, Flues and Vents** should be studied alongside fuel systems. Candidates should understand vent connectors, draft, clearances, terminations, appliance venting, and the safe removal of combustion products.

**Combustion Air** should be reviewed in relation to gas-fired appliances and equipment rooms. Candidates should understand why sufficient combustion air is required and how the code addresses openings and air supply.

**Hydronics** and **Chillers** should include pumps, valves, water flow, chilled-water distribution, heat transfer, controls, and the relationship between mechanical equipment and piping systems.

## More on test Information and Study Materials

**Ventilation and Exhausts** should include mechanical ventilation, exhaust systems, air movement, ducts, and equipment. The Uniform Mechanical Code should be used for targeted practice involving these requirements.

**Testing, Adjusting and Balancing** should include airflow measurement, system performance, equipment adjustment, commissioning concepts, and recognition of conditions that can prevent proper HVAC operation.

**Evaporative Cooling** should include basic operation, airflow, water distribution, pads or media, and system applications. Even though supplemental evaporative-cooling references are not included in this package, candidates should prepare for the subject because it appears on the exam.

**Solar** also appears on the exam. Candidates should understand solar equipment applications associated with air-temperature control within the scope of the C-21 classification.

**Safety** should be studied independently because technical safety knowledge may be tested even when a safety reference is not available in the exam room. HVAC and refrigeration contractors may work around electrical equipment, pressurized systems, ladders, tools, fuel gas, moving machinery, and mechanical rooms.

For open-book navigation, candidates should start with the table of contents and major chapters of both approved references. Then practice using the indexes, definitions, tables, and sections that relate to commonly tested subjects.

A useful study method is to sort practice questions into two groups: UMC-based topics and NFPA 54-based topics. Once candidates become comfortable choosing the correct reference, the questions can be mixed together to simulate the real exam.

Timed practice is essential. With 85 questions in three hours, candidates should learn when a lookup is worthwhile and when it is better to answer from trade knowledge and continue.

If a code search takes too long, move forward and return later when possible. Protecting overall exam time is more important than spending several minutes on one difficult question.

## How does the Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Contractor (C-21) exam work?

The Refrigeration and Air Conditioning Contractor (C-21) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

- Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
- The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
- PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
- The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
