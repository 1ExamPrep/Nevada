State Requirements

Nevada's B-2 Residential and Small Commercial classification covers construction and remodeling of houses and other structures that support, shelter, or enclose persons, animals, or property and that do not extend more than three stories above ground and one story below ground.

The classification is intended for general building work within those size limitations. Candidates should be prepared to understand both residential and small commercial construction because different project types may require different code references.

A B-2 contractor may coordinate multiple unrelated construction trades as part of a general building project. This can include sitework, concrete, structural framing, masonry, roofing, exterior protection, windows, doors, drywall, finishes, and other major phases.

The general building classification does not automatically authorize direct performance of every specialty trade. Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, fire protection, and other specialty work require the applicable authority when performed directly.

The B-2 trade examination is one part of the Nevada contractor licensing process. Applicants must also satisfy applicable experience, application, business and law, financial, and other licensing requirements before a contractor license is issued.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong B-2 study plan should combine construction knowledge with repeated use of the approved books. Candidates should become comfortable deciding whether a question belongs in the IBC, IRC, or ACI 318 before beginning a lookup.

Sitework preparation should include grading, excavation, soil conditions, drainage, trenches, compaction, and foundation preparation. Understanding how the site affects the structure is an important part of general building knowledge.

Concrete should include footings, foundation walls, slabs, reinforcement, formwork, placement, curing, and structural requirements. Candidates should practice using ACI 318 alongside the IBC and IRC so they understand where structural concrete provisions fit within the broader building code framework.

Masonry should include brick, block, stone, mortar, structural walls, openings, and the relationship between masonry and surrounding building systems.

Carpentry should include floor framing, wall framing, roof framing, sheathing, openings, headers, stairs, and construction sequencing. The IRC is particularly important for many residential framing requirements.

Thermal and Moisture Protection should include roofing, flashing, waterproofing, weather barriers, drainage, and insulation. Candidates should understand how these systems protect the building enclosure and prevent moisture-related problems.

Doors and Windows should be reviewed in relation to structural openings, installation, flashing, weather protection, and coordination with wall assemblies.

Plan Reading should be practiced regularly. Candidates should be able to read floor plans, foundation plans, elevations, sections, details, schedules, symbols, dimensions, and construction notes.

Estimating should include basic dimensions, areas, volumes, material quantities, and takeoffs. Accurate plan interpretation is important because estimating errors often begin with a missed dimension or misunderstood drawing detail.

The International Building Code should be studied by major subject area. Candidates should practice locating construction types, means of egress, structural requirements, fire and life safety provisions, and other broader building topics.

The International Residential Code should be used frequently for one- and two-family dwelling topics such as foundations, floors, wall framing, roofs, stairs, openings, and weather protection.

ACI 318-14 should be used for structural concrete topics. Candidates should learn its organization so reinforcement and concrete provisions can be located efficiently under exam conditions.

More on test Information and Study Materials

A practical preparation method is to separate questions by reference at first. Complete IBC questions, then IRC questions, then ACI 318 questions. Once candidates are familiar with the role of each book, mix the questions together so they must choose the correct reference before looking up the answer.

The highlighted and tabbed format can support this process by helping candidates return to major subjects quickly. However, the best preparation still includes understanding the structure of the book rather than relying only on tabs.

Definitions and tables should receive regular attention. Code questions often depend on exact terminology, and tables may include notes, footnotes, exceptions, or limitations that affect the correct answer.

Timed lookup practice is useful because the B-2 examination contains 80 questions in three hours. Candidates should become comfortable moving forward when a lookup takes too long and returning later when possible.

How does the Residential and Small Commercial Contractor (B-2) exam work?

The Residential and Small Commercial Contractor (B-2) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

  • Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
  • The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
  • PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
  • Nevada accepts a passing NASCLA Commercial General Building exam in place of the B trade exam, and since March 2026 issues a B-7 Restricted License for jobs up to $7,000.
