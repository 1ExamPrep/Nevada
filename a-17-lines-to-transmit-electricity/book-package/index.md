## Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada A-17 study plan should combine line-construction knowledge with construction-safety preparation and repeated closed-book practice.

**Overhead Lines** should be studied for poles, towers, crossarms, guys, anchors, conductors, insulators, transformers, hardware, and supporting equipment. Candidates should understand the purpose of each component and how the system is assembled.

**Underground Lines** should include underground cable systems, trenching, routing, protection, raceways, pulling methods, handling, and installation considerations. Underground electrical work also requires attention to excavation conditions and utility-location awareness.

**Poles and Structures** should be studied for support, stability, placement, hardware, guys, anchors, and equipment mounting. Candidates should understand how structural components support the electrical system and how field conditions can affect installation.

**Conductors and Cables** should include materials, handling, installation, connections, support, tension, identification, and application. Candidates should understand how overhead conductors differ from underground cables in both installation and protection.

**Transformers** should be reviewed for their function within electrical distribution systems. Candidates should understand how transformers support changes in voltage and how they connect with surrounding line equipment.

**Electrical Equipment** should include switches, protective devices, line hardware, distribution components, and other equipment used to control, support, or protect electrical systems.

**Line Construction Procedures** should be studied as sequences rather than isolated facts. Candidates should be able to describe how work is planned, how equipment is positioned, how conductors are installed, how components are supported, and how safety is maintained throughout the process.

**Fall Protection** should receive focused OSHA review because line work may involve elevated positions on poles, towers, structures, ladders, or aerial equipment. Candidates should understand the importance of proper access, positioning, and fall-prevention practices.

**Personal Protective Equipment** should be connected to actual hazards. Electrical exposure, tools, cutting, lifting, climbing, excavation, and material handling can all require appropriate protective equipment.

**Excavation Safety** should also be studied because underground line construction may involve trenches and other below-grade work. Candidates should understand basic excavation hazards and safe work practices.

**Tools and Equipment** should be studied by purpose and safe use. Line construction can require lifting equipment, pulling equipment, hand tools, climbing equipment, vehicles, and specialized utility tools.

**Material Handling** should include safe movement and storage of poles, reels, conductors, cable, transformers, hardware, and other heavy or awkward components. Candidates should understand how poor handling can create both safety problems and material damage.

Because the examination is closed book, active recall should be used after every major study session. Read a topic from Lineman’s and Cableman’s Handbook, close the book, and explain the system or procedure from memory.

For OSHA study, create short field scenarios. Ask what hazards are present, what controls are needed, and what safe work practice should be used. This helps turn regulations into practical knowledge that can be recalled on test day.

Practice questions should be used as diagnostic tools. If overhead line questions are difficult, return to poles, conductors, hardware, and line-construction topics. If underground questions are weak, review cable systems and excavation. If safety questions are consistently missed, increase OSHA review.

As preparation progresses, candidates should complete mixed practice sessions that combine line construction and safety. Real jobs do not separate these subjects, and the examination may require the candidate to apply technical knowledge and safety judgment within the same question.

A rotating study schedule can help keep preparation balanced. One session might focus on overhead systems, another on underground systems, another on transformers and equipment, and another on OSHA safety.

In the final stages of study, candidates should complete more review without opening either book. Closed-reference practice helps confirm whether terminology, equipment functions, procedures, and safety concepts can be recalled under exam conditions.

## How does the Lines to Transmit Electricity Contractor (A-17) exam work?

The Lines to Transmit Electricity Contractor (A-17) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

- Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
- The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
- PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
- The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
