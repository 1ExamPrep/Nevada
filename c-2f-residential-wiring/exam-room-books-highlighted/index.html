Exam Details

The Nevada C-2F Residential Wiring Contractor examination contains 50 questions. Candidates are allowed 2 hours to complete the examination and must answer 35 questions correctly to pass.

The examination content is divided into the following subject areas:

  • General Electrical Knowledge: 9 questions
  • Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits: 10 questions
  • Grounding and Bonding: 7 questions
  • Conductors and Cables: 7 questions
  • Overcurrent Protection: 4 questions
  • Electrical Power: 2 questions
  • Low Voltage: 2 questions
  • Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems: 2 questions
  • Safety Information: 4 questions
  • Raceways and Enclosures: 3 questions

Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits is the largest examination category with 10 questions. Candidates should understand service conductors, service equipment, disconnecting means, feeders, panelboards, branch circuits, load relationships, and common residential circuit requirements.

General Electrical Knowledge accounts for 9 questions and forms the foundation for many of the other categories. This area can involve electrical terminology, basic principles, circuit relationships, equipment, calculations, and code concepts.

Grounding and Bonding and Conductors and Cables each account for 7 questions. These two areas deserve substantial study time because they represent a significant percentage of the total examination.

Overcurrent Protection and Safety Information each account for 4 questions. Raceways and Enclosures accounts for 3 questions, while Electrical Power, Low Voltage, and Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems each account for 2 questions.

State Requirements

Nevada recognizes C-2F Residential Wiring as a subclassification within the C-2 Electrical classification.

The C-2F scope covers the installation, alteration, and repair of electrical wires, fixtures, appliances, and apparatuses whose electrical current does not exceed 600 volts on new or existing structures that do not exceed more than three stories above the ground.

This makes the classification residential in focus rather than an unrestricted electrical classification. Candidates should understand both the voltage limitation and the building-height limitation when evaluating the type of electrical work authorized under C-2F.

Residential wiring work can involve services, feeders, branch circuits, panelboards, grounding and bonding, raceways, cables, receptacles, switches, lighting, appliances, overcurrent devices, and other electrical apparatus within the authorized scope.

The classification requires an understanding of how different portions of a residential electrical system work together. Service equipment feeds distribution equipment, feeders supply panels and loads, branch circuits supply utilization equipment, and grounding and bonding help provide a safe fault-current path.

The C-2F trade examination is one part of the Nevada contractor licensing process. Applicants must also satisfy applicable qualification, application, business and law, financial, and other licensing requirements before a contractor license is issued.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada C-2F study plan should begin with Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits. Review service conductors, service equipment, disconnects, feeder arrangements, panelboards, branch-circuit ratings, loads, and common residential circuit configurations.

General Electrical Knowledge should include voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, electrical terminology, equipment, and the practical concepts needed to interpret NEC requirements.

Grounding and Bonding deserves significant attention. Candidates should understand grounding electrode systems, grounding electrode conductors, equipment grounding conductors, grounded conductors, bonding, and the purpose of providing an effective fault-current path.

Grounding terminology is especially important because several NEC terms sound similar but refer to different conductors or functions. Use the highlighted Article 100 definitions and grounding sections to reinforce those distinctions.

Conductors and Cables should include conductor materials, insulation types, ampacity, temperature ratings, conductor identification, cable assemblies, and sizing considerations.

The highlighted and tabbed NEC can support conductor study by making it easier to return to major tables and related provisions. Candidates should practice finding ampacity information, correction factors, adjustment factors, and notes that can affect conductor selection.

Overcurrent Protection should include circuit breakers, fuses, conductor protection, equipment protection, and the relationship between branch circuits, feeders, conductors, and overcurrent devices.

Raceways and Enclosures should include raceway types, boxes, fittings, support, physical protection, conductor fill, and basic installation requirements.

Electrical Power preparation should include basic electrical formulas and calculations. Candidates should become comfortable working through common electrical relationships without allowing calculation questions to consume excessive exam time.

Low Voltage and Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems should also be included in preparation. These categories are smaller, but each question still contributes toward the required passing score.

Safety Information should be treated as retained trade knowledge. Candidates should understand common electrical hazards, safe work practices, personal protective equipment, tools, ladders, temporary power conditions, and other construction safety concepts.

The 2017 National Electrical Code should be used during every study session. Start with the major structure of the book, then build familiarity with common residential wiring articles and tables.

Article 100 definitions should receive regular review. Electrical code questions often depend on recognizing the exact meaning of terms such as service, feeder, branch circuit, grounded conductor, grounding electrode conductor, and equipment grounding conductor.

More on test Information and Study Materials

Use the NEC index when the article number is not immediately known. Search for the key technical term, follow the reference, and then read the complete section rather than stopping at the first matching phrase.

Highlighted material can help direct attention to commonly studied requirements, while tabs can help candidates move between major sections quickly. The goal is not simply to know where a tab is located, but to understand why a particular article applies to the question.

A useful exercise is to choose a topic such as service disconnects, grounding electrodes, branch circuits, conductor ampacity, overcurrent protection, receptacles, boxes, or raceways and locate the applicable requirement under a time limit.

Repeat this process until navigating from the question to the correct section becomes more natural. The more familiar the book becomes during preparation, the less time is likely to be spent deciding where to begin a search.

Practice questions should initially be separated by subject. Work on services and feeders, then grounding, conductors, overcurrent protection, raceways, and other areas individually. This helps candidates identify weaker topics.

Once the individual categories are comfortable, move into mixed practice. The actual examination can switch rapidly between different subjects, so candidates should become comfortable moving from grounding to conductor sizing, services, raceways, or branch circuits without losing focus.

Time management should be included in every practice session. With 50 questions in two hours, spending several minutes on one code lookup can reduce the time available for easier questions.

A balanced open-book strategy is to answer familiar questions efficiently, use the highlighted and tabbed NEC for targeted confirmation, mark difficult items, and return to them after completing the easier questions.

How does the Residential Wiring Contractor (C-2F) exam work?

The Residential Wiring Contractor (C-2F) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

  • Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
  • The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
  • PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
  • The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
