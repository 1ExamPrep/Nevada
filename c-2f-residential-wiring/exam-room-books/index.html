Exam Details

The Nevada C-2F Residential Wiring Contractor examination contains 50 questions. Candidates are allowed 2 hours to complete the test and must answer 35 questions correctly to pass.

The examination content is divided into the following subject areas:

  • General Electrical Knowledge: 9 questions
  • Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits: 10 questions
  • Grounding and Bonding: 7 questions
  • Conductors and Cables: 7 questions
  • Overcurrent Protection: 4 questions
  • Electrical Power: 2 questions
  • Low Voltage: 2 questions
  • Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems: 2 questions
  • Safety Information: 4 questions
  • Raceways and Enclosures: 3 questions

Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits is the largest section of the examination with 10 questions. Candidates should understand service equipment, disconnecting means, feeder arrangements, branch circuits, load relationships, panelboards, and common residential wiring requirements.

General Electrical Knowledge accounts for 9 questions. This area includes the foundational principles and terminology needed to work through the rest of the exam.

Grounding and Bonding and Conductors and Cables each account for 7 questions. Together, these categories represent a significant portion of the examination and deserve substantial preparation time.

Overcurrent Protection and Safety Information each account for 4 questions. Raceways and Enclosures accounts for 3 questions, while Electrical Power, Low Voltage, and Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems each contribute 2 questions.

State Requirements

Nevada recognizes C-2F Residential Wiring as a subclassification within the C-2 Electrical classification.

The C-2F scope covers the installation, alteration, and repair of electrical wires, fixtures, appliances, and apparatuses whose electrical current does not exceed 600 volts on new or existing structures that do not exceed more than three stories above the ground.

The scope makes the C-2F classification focused on residential electrical work rather than unrestricted electrical contracting. Candidates should understand both the voltage limitation and building-height limitation when evaluating the authorized work.

Residential wiring work can involve service equipment, feeders, branch circuits, panelboards, grounding and bonding, raceways, cables, receptacles, switches, lighting, appliances, overcurrent devices, and other electrical equipment within the authorized scope.

Because residential electrical work affects life safety and property protection, candidates should understand how code requirements for conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, grounding, bonding, wiring methods, boxes, and equipment installation work together.

The C-2F trade examination is only one part of the Nevada contractor licensing process. Applicants must also satisfy applicable qualification, application, business and law, financial, and other licensing requirements before a contractor license is issued.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada C-2F preparation plan should begin with Service, Feeders, and Branch Circuits, the largest exam category. Review service conductors, service equipment, disconnecting means, feeder requirements, branch-circuit ratings, panelboards, loads, and common residential circuit arrangements.

General Electrical Knowledge should include voltage, current, resistance, power, electrical terminology, circuit relationships, and the practical concepts needed to understand NEC requirements.

Grounding and Bonding deserves substantial attention. Candidates should understand grounding electrode systems, grounding electrode conductors, equipment grounding conductors, grounded conductors, bonding requirements, and the purpose of creating an effective fault-current path.

Grounding terminology is especially important because similar-sounding terms can have different meanings. Review Article 100 definitions and the applicable grounding and bonding provisions until those distinctions become familiar.

Conductors and Cables should include conductor materials, insulation types, ampacity, temperature ratings, conductor identification, cable assemblies, allowable uses, and sizing requirements.

Candidates should practice finding conductor information in the NEC tables. Learn how to recognize when adjustment factors, correction factors, temperature ratings, or other conditions can affect conductor selection.

Overcurrent Protection should include circuit breakers, fuses, conductor protection, equipment protection, branch-circuit relationships, and the role of overcurrent protective devices in residential electrical systems.

Raceways and Enclosures should include conduit and raceway types, boxes, fittings, support, protection from physical damage, conductor fill, and installation requirements.

Electrical Power preparation should include common electrical formulas and relationships. Candidates should be comfortable performing basic calculations without spending excessive time during the exam.

Low Voltage should be reviewed because the examination includes questions in this area. Understand the basic differences between low-voltage systems and conventional power wiring.

Fire and Intrusion Detection and Alarm Systems should also be part of preparation. Candidates should understand basic residential alarm-system wiring concepts and electrical relationships.

Safety Information should be studied as retained knowledge. Candidates should understand safe electrical work practices, electrical hazards, personal protective equipment, tools, ladders, temporary power, and general construction safety principles.

The 2017 National Electrical Code should be used during every study session. Rather than reading the book from beginning to end, organize review around the examination subjects and the NEC articles most closely associated with them.

More on test Information and Study Materials

Begin by learning the overall structure of the NEC. Chapter 1 contains general requirements, Chapter 2 focuses heavily on wiring and protection, Chapter 3 addresses wiring methods and materials, and later chapters cover equipment and special applications.

The NEC index is an important navigation tool. When the correct article is not immediately obvious, identify the technical term in the question and use the index to locate the likely section.

Article 100 definitions should also receive regular review. Electrical code questions often depend on knowing how a term is defined before the correct requirement can be applied.

Candidates should learn to read beyond a single sentence. NEC requirements can contain exceptions, subsections, tables, notes, and conditions that change how a general rule applies.

A useful study exercise is to write down a residential wiring topic such as service disconnects, grounding electrodes, receptacle circuits, conductor ampacity, raceway fill, or overcurrent protection and locate the applicable section under a time limit.

Repeat the lookup until the path through the index, article, and subsection becomes familiar. This kind of repeated navigation can improve efficiency without requiring candidates to memorize every code section number.

Practice questions should be organized first by subject. Work through services and feeders separately from grounding, conductors, raceways, and overcurrent protection. Once those areas are comfortable, move into mixed practice.

Mixed practice is valuable because the actual examination can move quickly from one electrical subject to another. Candidates should be prepared to switch from a grounding question to a conductor table, then to a branch-circuit requirement or a raceway rule.

Time management should be built into preparation. With 50 questions in two hours, candidates should avoid spending several minutes searching for one difficult answer while easier questions remain incomplete.

A balanced strategy is to answer familiar trade questions efficiently, use the NEC for targeted verification, flag difficult questions, and return to them after completing the easier portions of the exam.

How does the Residential Wiring Contractor (C-2F) exam work?

The Residential Wiring Contractor (C-2F) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

  • Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
  • The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
  • PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
  • The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
