## State Requirements

Nevada designates **C-20** as the specialty classification for **Tiling**.

A Nevada C-20 contractor may install, by means of suction and adhesives, **ceramic tile, encaustic tile, vitreous tile, and other tile-like products**, except for hollow or structural partitioning tile.

The C-20 classification therefore focuses on surface preparation and tile installation rather than structural partition tile work. Candidates should understand how tile products are installed over suitable surfaces and how substrate conditions, bonding materials, and finishing procedures affect the completed installation.

The C-20 classification also includes designated subclassifications covering plastic tile and wallboard and swimming pool tile and coping. Candidates seeking the full C-20 classification should prepare for the scope applicable to the broader tiling specialty.

Tile contractors must understand the relationship among the substrate, setting material, tile product, grout, movement considerations, and finished surface. A problem with one part of that system can affect the entire installation.

The C-20 trade examination is one part of Nevada contractor licensing. Applicants must also satisfy applicable qualification, application, business, financial, and administrative requirements before a contractor license is issued.

## Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada C-20 study plan should begin with **Surface Preparation**. Tile installations depend on the quality and suitability of the surface receiving the tile, so candidates should understand how to inspect, prepare, clean, level, and evaluate substrates before setting work begins.

Study common floor and wall conditions that can affect tile installation. Candidates should understand why flatness, cleanliness, stability, and surface condition matter to proper bonding.

The **Builders Guide to Floors, 1997** can help reinforce knowledge of floor construction, subfloor systems, underlayments, leveling, and support conditions. Understanding the floor beneath the tile can help candidates recognize installation problems before tile is placed.

**Moisture** should be considered during surface preparation because inappropriate moisture conditions can affect setting materials, substrates, and completed installations.

**Surface cleaning** is another basic but important topic. Dust, debris, coatings, contaminants, and other conditions can interfere with bonding between the tile-setting material and the substrate.

**Installation and Adhesives** deserves the greatest study emphasis because it represents 22 questions, more than half of the examination.

Candidates should understand the differences among common setting methods and the role of mortars, adhesives, and related bonding products. Focus on matching the setting material to the tile, substrate, and installation environment.

**Setting Tile, 1995 (USED)** can help candidates study practical tile installation techniques, including layout, cutting, fitting, mortar application, adhesive use, setting, and finishing.

**Layout** is a core tiling skill. Candidates should understand how tile placement affects appearance, cuts, joints, transitions, and the overall balance of the finished installation.

Practice working through layout decisions mentally. Consider how a room, wall, opening, corner, or obstruction can affect starting points and cut-tile placement.

**Cutting and fitting** should also receive attention. Tile frequently must be fitted around edges, corners, penetrations, fixtures, and other building components.

**Mortar application** is important because proper contact between the tile, setting material, and substrate contributes to a reliable installation. Candidates should understand appropriate application practices and why consistent coverage matters.

**Adhesive selection** should be studied in relation to the tile product, substrate, and installation condition. Different materials and environments may require different setting products.

The **ANSI A108/A118/A136.1:2017** reference can help candidates understand recognized standards governing installation methods and setting materials. Use it to connect practical field techniques with formal industry requirements.

## More on test Information and Study Materials

Rather than trying to memorize every standard number, focus on the practical purpose of the requirements. Understand why certain materials, preparation methods, or installation practices are used and what problem each requirement is designed to prevent.

**Tile spacing and joints** should also be studied. Candidates should understand how consistent joints contribute to appearance and how joint planning relates to tile dimensions and the finished surface.

**Movement** within the substrate or tile assembly can affect installations. Candidates should understand why tile systems must accommodate expected building and material movement.

**Grout and Finishing** represents 6 questions and should not be neglected. Review grout preparation, placement, joint filling, cleanup, final surface treatment, and finishing procedures.

Candidates should understand why the grout must be properly worked into the joints and why timing can affect cleanup and the finished appearance.

**Grout joints** should be studied as part of the complete installation rather than as a final cosmetic step. Joint condition, depth, cleanliness, and material selection can affect performance.

**Finishing** should include attention to the completed surface, joint appearance, cleanup, transitions, edges, and other details that affect professional workmanship.

Because the examination is closed book, candidates should convert the information in the references into retained knowledge. Active recall is particularly useful for procedures that follow a sequence.

For example, study a floor tile installation and then explain the entire process from memory: inspect the substrate, prepare the surface, establish the layout, select the setting material, spread the mortar or adhesive, place and align the tile, allow the installation to develop appropriately, grout the joints, and complete the finishing work.

Another useful exercise is to compare installation conditions. Ask how the approach might change based on the substrate, tile type, setting material, or location.

Create separate study sessions for Surface Preparation, Installation and Adhesives, and Grout and Finishing. Because the exam weighting is not equal, allocate the most time to Installation and Adhesives.

A practical study schedule might spend more than half of trade-preparation time on installation and bonding concepts, followed by concentrated surface-preparation review and regular grout and finishing practice.

Once individual topics are familiar, move into mixed practice. The actual examination may move from a substrate question to an adhesive question and then to grout or finishing without warning.

## How does the Tiling Contractor (C-20) exam work?

The Tiling Contractor (C-20) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

- Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
- The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
- PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
- The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
