## State Requirements

Nevada designates **C-20** as the specialty classification for **Tiling**.

A Nevada C-20 contractor may install, by means of suction and adhesives, **ceramic tile, encaustic tile, vitreous tile, and other tile-like products**, except hollow or structural partitioning tile.

The C-20 classification therefore focuses on tile installation and related surface preparation rather than structural partition tile work. Candidates should understand the relationship between substrate preparation, setting materials, tile products, joints, grout, and finishing.

Tile installations depend on the full system working together. Poor substrate preparation can affect bonding. Incorrect adhesive selection can affect performance. Improper layout can create difficult cuts or poor appearance. Inadequate grout and finishing practices can affect both performance and final presentation.

The C-20 trade examination is one part of Nevada contractor licensing. Applicants must also satisfy applicable qualification, application, business, financial, and administrative requirements before a contractor license is issued.

## Test Information and Study Materials

A strong Nevada C-20 study plan should begin with **Surface Preparation**. Candidates should understand how to evaluate the condition of a floor or wall before tile installation begins and why preparation directly affects bonding and finished performance.

**Substrates** should be reviewed carefully. Candidates should understand how different surface conditions can affect installation and why a stable, properly prepared surface is important.

**Leveling and flatness** should also be part of preparation. Irregular surfaces can make installation more difficult and can affect tile alignment, joint consistency, and finished appearance.

The **Builders Guide to Floors, 1997** can help candidates study floor construction, subfloors, underlayments, support conditions, and surface preparation. The highlighted and tabbed format can make it easier to move among these subjects during study.

**Moisture conditions** should be reviewed because moisture can affect substrates, adhesives, mortars, and the completed tile assembly.

**Surface cleanliness** is another important area. Dust, debris, residue, coatings, or other contaminants can interfere with proper bonding between the substrate and setting material.

**Installation and Adhesives** should receive the greatest amount of study time because it represents more than half of the examination.

The **Setting Tile, 1995 (USED)** reference can help candidates review practical installation methods, tile layout, cutting, fitting, mortar application, adhesive use, tile placement, grout, and finishing.

**Layout** is central to tile work. Candidates should understand how room dimensions, walls, openings, corners, and obstructions can affect starting points, tile placement, joint alignment, and cut-tile locations.

Practice visualizing an installation before setting begins. Consider how the layout will look across the full surface and where cuts will fall at edges, walls, and transitions.

**Cutting and fitting** should be studied in relation to corners, edges, penetrations, fixtures, transitions, and other conditions that require tile to be adjusted before placement.

**Mortars and adhesives** deserve careful attention because selecting the correct setting material is essential to a successful installation. Candidates should understand how the tile, substrate, and installation environment influence material selection.

**Coverage** should be understood as part of proper bonding. Candidates should know why consistent contact between the tile, setting material, and substrate is important to installation quality.

The **ANSI A108/A118/A136.1:2017** reference can help candidates connect practical installation techniques with recognized industry standards for ceramic tile work.

## More on test Information and Study Materials

Rather than trying to memorize every standard number, candidates should focus on understanding what the standards are intended to accomplish. Learn how the requirements relate to installation methods, setting materials, adhesives, and tile performance.

**Material compatibility** is another important area. Candidates should understand that not every setting product is appropriate for every tile or substrate condition.

**Tile spacing and joints** should also be reviewed. Consistent joints contribute to appearance and can affect how the finished installation accommodates movement and variation.

**Movement considerations** should be studied as part of the complete tile system. Buildings and materials can move, expand, or contract, and the installation must be able to accommodate expected conditions.

**Grout and Finishing** represents a smaller portion of the examination but remains important. Candidates should understand grout preparation, application, joint filling, cleanup, edge finishing, and final surface treatment.

**Grout joints** should be viewed as part of the installation system rather than merely a cosmetic feature. Joint condition, cleanliness, depth, and material selection can affect both appearance and performance.

**Cleanup** should be studied in relation to timing and completed appearance. Proper finishing requires attention to both the tile surface and the joints.

The highlighted and tabbed references can help candidates divide preparation by topic. One section of study might focus on substrate preparation, another on setting materials, another on layout and cutting, and another on grout and finishing.

Because the examination is closed book, candidates should gradually reduce their dependence on the references as test day approaches. Early study sessions can use the tabs and highlighting heavily, while later sessions should focus more on recall and application.

A useful active-recall exercise is to study a complete floor tile installation and then describe the process without looking at the books: inspect the substrate, prepare the surface, establish the layout, select the proper setting material, spread the material, place and align the tile, allow the installation to progress properly, grout the joints, and complete final finishing.

Another useful exercise is to compare two different installation conditions and explain how the substrate or setting material might affect the approach.

Practice questions should initially be grouped by category so weak areas are easier to identify. Later, move into mixed practice covering Surface Preparation, Installation and Adhesives, and Grout and Finishing together.

## How does the Tiling Contractor (C-20) exam work?

The Tiling Contractor (C-20) license comes from the Nevada State Contractors Board. You apply to the NSCB with your experience and financial statement, the Board sends an exam eligibility letter, and you book the CMS and the trade exam with PSI. Scores come right after the exam.

- Every Nevada contractor license needs the Business and Law (CMS) exam plus a trade exam for the classification, both given by PSI.
- The CMS is open book with the Construction Business and Law Manual for Nevada; trade exams are closed book except for the code references their outline allows.
- PSI charges $95 for one exam portion, or $140 for a trade exam taken with the CMS, and you get three attempts before the application is void.
- The Board sets a bond from $1,000 to $500,000 and a monetary limit on every license, based on your experience and financial statements.
